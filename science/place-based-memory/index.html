Place-based memory — why location is the brain's favourite anchor

By F. Klepper · Murnau am Staffelsee

You can probably remember exactly where you were when you heard certain news. A phone call that changed something. A conversation that surprised you. The moment before, the room or street or car seat — still clear.

You may not remember what you had for breakfast on the same day.

This asymmetry isn't random. Location is one of the brain's most reliable memory anchors — and it has been understood, in practical if not scientific terms, for roughly 2,500 years.

The oldest memory technique we know

Around 500 BCE, the Greek lyric poet Simonides of Ceos reportedly survived the collapse of a banqueting hall in Thessaly. When the rubble was cleared, the bodies were too badly damaged to identify. Simonides was able to name them all — by remembering where each guest had been seated.

He is credited, in the accounts that followed, with identifying what this implies: that location is an unusually powerful hook for memory. If you want to remember something, attach it to a place.

The technique that came from this insight is called the method of loci. You mentally place the items you want to remember at specific locations along a familiar route — your home, a street you know well, a building you've walked through many times. To recall them, you mentally retrace the route. The items are waiting where you left them.

The Rhetorica ad Herennium, a Roman rhetorical manual from around 86 BCE, describes the technique in careful detail. Cicero used it. Medieval scholars used it. Memory competition champions still use it today. The underlying principle — that spatial context supports memory retrieval — has been a practical tool for longer than most intellectual traditions.

What changed in the late twentieth century is that neuroscience caught up with an explanation for why it works.

Place cells and the brain's internal map

In 1971, neuroscientist John O'Keefe, working at University College London, discovered something unexpected in the hippocampi of rats navigating a space. Certain neurons fired only when the animal was in a specific location. Move the rat to a different spot, different neurons fired. These cells formed a kind of internal map — each one corresponding to a particular place in the environment. O'Keefe called them place cells.

Decades later, May-Britt Moser and Edvard Moser at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology discovered a related system in a neighbouring brain region, the entorhinal cortex. They found neurons that fire in a regular hexagonal grid pattern as an animal moves through space — effectively providing a coordinate system that the place cells can use. These are grid cells, identified in 2005.

In 2014, O'Keefe and the Mosers shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this work. The prize citation described their discoveries as cells that "constitute a positioning system in the brain" — an inner GPS.

What's relevant for memory is that the hippocampus, which contains the place cells, is also the brain's central structure for forming and retrieving episodic memories — memories of specific events in your life. This isn't a coincidence. The same structure that tracks where you are also tracks what happened there. Spatial context and event memory are processed together, in the same region, from the start.

Why context shapes what you can recall

The practical consequence of this architecture is that the environment at retrieval matters.

In 1975, psychologists Duncan Godden and Alan Baddeley conducted a study with deep-sea divers. Participants learned lists of words either underwater or on land, then were tested for recall either in the same environment or the opposite one. Recall was substantially better when the environment at testing matched the environment at learning. The context wasn't just a backdrop — it was part of the memory itself.

This is context-dependent memory: what you learned somewhere is more accessible when you return there. Not just physically, but also mentally. Imagining yourself back in a place can serve as a retrieval cue almost as effectively as being there.

Eleanor Maguire, also at University College London, extended this line of research in a different direction. Studying London taxi drivers — who spend years acquiring detailed knowledge of the city's streets — she found that their posterior hippocampi were measurably larger than those of non-taxi drivers, and that the size correlated with years of experience. The brain region that handles spatial memory was physically changed by sustained navigational use. Spatial memory isn't a fixed capacity. It responds to practice.

What this means in practice

The method of loci works because it exploits a feature of how the brain already organises experience. You're not forcing an artificial association. You're using a system the hippocampus runs automatically — the linking of events to the places where they occur.

You don't need to construct elaborate memory palaces to benefit from this. The simpler version is already happening whenever you're paying enough attention to notice where you are.

A few things I've found that follow from this:

Capture place when it matters. If something happens that you want to remember — a conversation, a decision, a moment — note where you were. Not as an abstract tag, but as a real detail. The street, the room, the specific spot. That detail becomes a retrieval handle.

Use return visits deliberately. Going back to a place where something significant happened isn't nostalgia — it's activating a context that your memory is already indexed to. The location brings information with it that pure recall often can't access.

Imagine the place when recalling. If you're trying to remember something and it's not coming, try reconstructing the physical environment first. Where were you sitting? What could you see? This isn't a guarantee, but it's a genuine technique with a neurological basis.

A personal note

I've known for a long time that certain places carry memories for me with unusual vividness. A specific corner in Vienna where I spent an afternoon during a particular period of my life. A café in a Bavarian town where I had a conversation that I still return to. I'd assumed this was something personal — some quirk of how I happened to be wired.

What the research made clear is that it isn't personal at all. The place-memory link is structural. The hippocampus builds it automatically. The people for whom places don't trigger memories aren't wired differently — they just haven't been somewhere meaningful enough times, or haven't been paying attention when they were.

The practical upshot was that I started treating places as memory infrastructure rather than backdrop. Noting where I was became as important as noting what happened. The two are encoded together. Retrieving one retrieves the other.
