Activity tracking beyond fitness: why your hobbies deserve data too

By RememberApps Studio · Bavarian Alps

My phone knows exactly how many steps I took last Tuesday.

It does not know that I finished a book I'd been reading for three weeks. It does not know I went to a concert, or that I cycled to the lake on Saturday morning, or that I spent a quiet evening cooking something that actually worked. None of that is in any app. None of it gets counted.

This seems like a strange omission. Steps are the least interesting thing that happened to me last week.

What life logging actually is

Fitness tracking is a subset of something broader — the practice of recording what you do with your time. Life logging, as it's sometimes called, covers the full range: not just exercise, but reading, watching, attending, visiting, making, resting.

The quantified self movement has been around since at least 2007, when Wired editors Gary Wolf and Kevin Kelly coined the term. The original vision was ambitious — sensors, spreadsheets, self-knowledge through data. In practice, most people who tried it ended up tracking steps and sleep and quietly abandoning everything else.

That's a shame, because the interesting part was never the biometrics.

The interesting part was the question underneath: what am I actually doing with my days? Not how many calories burned. Not how many hours slept. But what did I do? What did I experience? What happened that I'll want to remember?

That's the version of tracking that I've found worth doing.

The gap between fitness apps and the rest of your life

Fitness apps are good at what they're built for. Strava records your ride. Apple Health counts your steps. Garmin logs your heart rate. These tools are precise, automatic, and designed for measurable, repeatable activities.

But most of what makes up a life isn't measurable or repeatable. A concert happens once. The book you read this month is different from the one you read last month. The conversation at dinner, the walk through a neighbourhood you'd never been to, the afternoon you spent doing absolutely nothing — none of this fits into a fitness app's data model.

And so it goes unrecorded. Not because it doesn't matter. Because no one built a tracker for it.

The result is a strange asymmetry. We have years of data on how fast we ran. We have nothing on what we were reading during those years, or where we travelled, or which evenings we'll still remember in a decade.

What's actually worth tracking

The categories I've settled on, after a few years of trial and error:

Things you attended. Concerts, films, theatre, talks, sports events. One line: what it was, who you were with, one word on how it was. This takes thirty seconds and you have a record of your cultural life that no streaming service can give you back.

Things you read. Not just titles — a short note on why you picked it up and what stuck. This is the part most reading apps skip. Goodreads will tell you that you read 22 books last year. It won't tell you which one you've thought about every week since.

Places you went. Not just restaurants — hiking trails, neighbourhoods, cities, the viewpoint you found by accident. The Places hub covers this in more depth, but the principle is the same: location is a strong memory anchor, and recording it while you're there costs almost nothing.

Things you made or did. Cooked something new. Fixed something. Finished a project. These are easy to forget because they're not performative — nobody else saw them — but they're often what you're most proud of, in retrospect.

Quiet evenings worth remembering. This one surprised me when I started. Some of the most valuable entries in my log are from evenings where nothing happened. Reading, cooking, a walk. But the specific combination of those things, on that particular night, in that particular period of your life — that's irreplaceable. And it vanishes completely if you don't catch it.

How to track without it becoming a compulsion

The risk with any tracking practice is that it starts to feel like an obligation. You miss a day and feel behind. You start recording things not because they matter but because the habit demands it. The log becomes a task instead of a record.

I've been there. Here's what helped.

No minimums. Some weeks I log four or five things. Some weeks I log nothing. The practice doesn't require consistency — it requires lowering the threshold for what counts as worth recording.

Meaning over metrics. The question I ask is not "did I do this?" but "do I want to remember this?" That reframe changes what gets logged. I'm not building a database of my activity. I'm building a record of what mattered.

One line is enough. The entry doesn't need to be complete. "Finished the Chekhov stories. Better than I remembered." That's sufficient. Future me will know what I mean. The goal is a trigger for memory, not a comprehensive account.

Don't optimise. The moment I start reviewing categories, calculating how many books per month, comparing weeks — the practice has tipped into something else. I use my log for recall, not analysis. What did I do in November? What was I reading in the spring? Those are the questions it answers.

What surprised me when I started

I expected tracking to make me more aware of how I was spending time. It did, but not in the way I'd assumed.

I didn't suddenly make better choices or fill my evenings with improving activities. What happened was simpler and more useful: I stopped forgetting things I'd actually done.

There's a particular phenomenon where a good experience leaves almost no trace if nothing fixes it in memory. You see a great film and three months later you can't remember whether you actually watched it or just meant to. You go to a concert and six months later the details are gone — the setlist, the venue, the friend you went with, what the night felt like.

The log doesn't fix your memory. It extends it. The entries are small. The effect, over years, is that your past becomes more present. You can look back and see what you were actually doing, not just a vague sense that time passed.

I find this valuable in a way that's hard to explain precisely. It's not nostalgia. It's more like continuity. The sense that your life has a shape you can actually see.

I'll mention, briefly: the reason I built Remember was partly this — I wanted a single place for activities, places, and notes that worked the way I think about these things, with location as the organiser rather than category. It's an iOS app, at the bottom of the Activities hub if you want to look. But any consistent system will do. The medium matters much less than the habit.
