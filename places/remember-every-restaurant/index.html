Remember every restaurant you visit — and find your favourites again

By F. Klepper · Murnau am Staffelsee

There's a specific kind of frustration I've felt more times than I'd like to admit.

You're planning a trip back to a city you visited two years ago. There was a place — a small restaurant, not on any list, the kind you find by walking. The pasta was made that morning. The room was narrow and loud. You sat there for two hours and didn't want to leave.

You cannot remember the name. You cannot remember the street. You remember the experience perfectly — the light, the feeling, the food — and none of the information that would let you go back.

This happens because of a gap between how our memory works and what we actually need from it.

Why we forget places we loved

Spatial memory is one of the oldest and most reliable systems in the human brain. We remember where things were with remarkable precision — the layout of a room, the turn you take to reach a specific café, the way a particular street opens onto a square. This isn't coincidence. For most of human history, knowing where food, water, and shelter were located was more important than knowing their names.

But names are exactly what we need to find places again in the modern world. And names are exactly what spatial memory doesn't reliably store.

What we remember is the experience — sensory, emotional, contextual. The weight of the menu. The particular blue of the tiles. The feeling of having been somewhere good. What we forget is the label attached to it. The noun. The thing you'd type into a search bar.

This is why "it was somewhere near the market" is the most common last known location of a restaurant you loved.

When to write it down

The window is short. Everything I know about capturing memories suggests the same thing: same day or never.

Sleep is not passive. During the night, the brain consolidates what it decides to keep and discards the rest. A restaurant you visited and didn't record before bed is a restaurant your memory has already started to degrade. By the following week, you'll have the emotional residue — that was a good one — with none of the specifics.

This isn't a reason to be anxious about it. It's just a reason to be practical. Writing down a restaurant takes less time than finishing your dessert. The question is whether you make it a habit at all.

My rule: if I want to remember a place, I note it before I leave the neighbourhood. Not the next day. Not at home that evening. In the moment, or close enough to it that the details are still sharp.

What's actually worth noting

Not everything. Here's what I've found useful, after years of experimenting with more and less:

Name and location. The obvious two. If you can't find it again, none of the rest matters. A screenshot of the pin on your map is often the fastest option.

What you ordered. Specifically the thing that made it worth remembering. Not "pasta" — the pici with truffle, or whatever it was. Future you will want to order it again.

Who you were with. Restaurant memories are almost always social memories. The companion matters as much as the food, and noting it will bring the whole memory back in a way that a dish name alone won't.

A one-line reason. Why was this one worth remembering? "Best focaccia I've had in five years" or "exactly the kind of neighbourhood place I can never find when I want one" — something that explains your rating to yourself.

A rating, if you use them. I use a simple three-tier: would return, would recommend, wouldn't go back. No decimals, no overthinking. The goal isn't a database. It's a reminder system for yourself.

That's the whole thing. Five fields, most of which you fill in seconds.

Building something you can actually use

The list is only as useful as your ability to search it. This is where most people's informal approaches break down — a note here, a screenshot there, a saved Instagram post that you'll never look at again.

What I wanted was something closer to a personal map. Not just a list of restaurants, but a visual record of where I'd been — one that I could open when I'm in a city and see, at a glance, what I'd loved nearby. Or what I'd thought was overrated. Or where I'd had a first date, or a last meal before a flight.

The medium matters here. Saving restaurants as bookmarks gives you a list. Saving them on a map gives you context. When you're standing in a neighbourhood, a map tells you something useful. A list doesn't.

I built my first rough version of this in a spreadsheet, then a notes app, then eventually in the app I made. But any consistent system works — even a notes app with location tags and a naming convention you'll recognise later. The system beats the tool.

Favourites and the return visit

The other use case I keep coming back to: finding your favourites when you actually need them.

I have a handful of restaurants I'd call reliable. Places I know. Places I'd take someone I wanted to impress, or places I'd go alone on a quiet evening and feel at home. Maintaining this list — actually knowing what's on it — turns out to require some maintenance.

People stop going to places they loved, not because the places changed, but because they forgot they existed. A friend opens a new recommendation and you're there, except there was already a version of this you'd found two years ago that was better.

Having a searchable personal record means you stop defaulting to whatever's new and visible. You can search "Italian, Vienna" or "good for a long lunch, Munich" and get back results that are actually calibrated to your taste — because they came from you.

This is the thing that surprised me most, when I started keeping track properly. I didn't just remember more places. I returned to them.

A note on maps

I've tried a lot of approaches to this over the years. Saved pins on Google Maps worked for a while — until I had 300 of them and couldn't tell which ones I'd actually liked. Notes apps worked until the notes became unsearchable. A dedicated travel journal worked until I stopped carrying it.

What stuck was a map that was also a log. Where location was the primary organiser, and everything else — the notes, the rating, the photo — was attached to the pin. That's how I think about restaurants anyway. Not as items on a list, but as places I've been.

I'll mention briefly: this is what I eventually built Remember for. It's an iOS app I made for exactly this use case — keeping a personal map of places with notes and ratings. It's at the bottom of the Places hub if you're curious. But the principle works in any app that lets you combine location with notes.

What I actually do now

For reference: when I arrive somewhere I want to remember, I open the app, drop a pin, type the name and one line, and add a photo if there's something worth showing. It takes about ninety seconds.

I review the map occasionally — when I'm planning a trip, or when someone asks for recommendations in a city I know. The map is full of places that, without this habit, I would have completely forgotten.

Last year I went back to a restaurant in Lisbon I'd marked four years earlier. I had the table I wanted, ordered the dish I'd noted, and remembered why I'd wanted to return. That's the whole point. Small effort at the time. Actual return on it later.

The goal isn't completeness. You don't need to track every meal. You just need to catch the ones you'll regret losing.
